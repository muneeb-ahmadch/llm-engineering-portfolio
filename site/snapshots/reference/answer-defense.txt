Reference · RAG · the defense layer Closing the answer gap RAG never scores 1.0. You don't fix that, you make it fail safe and measurable . Every gap between your golden set and perfect gets a named control. Compressed from Lesson 09. Made to be reread the night before. The premise: no RAG system is ever fully correct Your own numbers say so. Six lessons of measurement landed at MRR 0.785, 2/20 misses , and Day 16's tuned baseline peaked at 0.790. That is the ceiling honest RAG lives under: “Anyone claiming RAG metrics near 1.0 is either overfitting the golden set or not measuring what they think” ( rag-metrics ). So the engineering question is never “how do I get every answer right?” It is: when the system is wrong: and it will be, does it fail loudly and safely, or silently and dangerously? The controls below are how you buy the first over the second. What generation adds: two new ways to be wrong Retrieval-only (Lessons 01–06) had exactly one failure: the right chunk isn't in the top-k. The moment an LLM enters the loop (Lesson 07), two failures appear that no retrieval metric can see , both of which you already produced: Failure What it looks like Your evidence Ungrounded & wrong (hallucination) The answer states a fact that's in none of the retrieved chunks, and it's false. The grounding instruction was ignored. The “Paris” probe: context was an LSTM chunk, instruction said abstain, model answered “Paris, the capital of France.” Ungrounded & lucky (parametric rescue) The answer is correct but came from the model's pre-training, not the context. Fine today; a landmine on a private corpus where the model has no fallback. Both retrieval MISSes got textbook-correct answers, because overfitting / reranking are generic ML the model knew cold. Why “lucky” is the more dangerous one A hallucination on a public fact you can catch by eye. But parametric rescue passes every test you're watching, the answer is right: so you ship it, then deploy on InsureElm's proprietary policies where the model has never seen the answer, and the exact same retrieval miss that looked harmless now returns a confident fabrication. This is why Lesson 04 kept retrieval and answer metrics separate: answer quality on a public corpus flatters you. Keep measuring retrieval on its own, and never trust a right answer whose chunk you can't point to. The defense ladder: one control per gap Cheapest first. Each catches something the one above it can't. You don't need all six on day one; you need to know which gap each one closes. # Control Catches Cost 1 Grounded prompt Some hallucination. Ops time 1 · Grounded prompting: necessary, never sufficient Your harness already does this: “Use the provided context… If you don't know, say 'I don't have that information.'” Keep it: a model told to ground itself hallucinates less . But it is a nudge on a probability distribution, not a lock. The Paris probe is the proof: same prompt, model reached past the context anyway because it “knew” the answer. Treat grounded prompting as the floor, then verify that it held, don't assume it. 2 · Abstention as a first-class answer “I don't have that information” is not a failure, on a question the corpus can't answer, it is the only correct output . So make it measurable. Abstention rate = fraction of answers that decline. Read it as a band, not a target: abstention too LOW → the model is answering things it shouldn't (hallucinating) abstention too HIGH → uselessly timid; retrieval or threshold is starving it A system that never abstains on a hard corpus isn't confident, it's lying somewhere you haven't looked. 3 · Citations: make the lie visible Require the model to tag each claim with the chunk it came from ( [source: day26] ). Two payoffs: a citation that doesn't actually support its claim is a caught hallucination (you or a judge can check it), and the reader gets a verification path instead of a black box. Forcing attribution also empirically pulls answers back toward the context. 4 · Faithfulness: the metric that catches the hallucination The one rag-metrics said to remember, now operationalized. An LLM judge reads the answer and only its retrieved context, and scores: is every claim in the answer entailed by the context? A claim that's true-but-ungrounded still fails. That's the point, it catches both the Paris fabrication and the parametric rescue, neither of which any retrieval metric or gold-answer match can see. Property Why it matters Reference-free Needs no gold answer: just answer + context. So it scales to questions you never hand-labelled. Judge ≥ generator A weaker judge rubber-stamps. Use a model at least as strong as the one that wrote the answer. Structured output Force a parseable verdict (score or claim-by-claim yes/no), or you can't aggregate or gate on it. Non-deterministic The judge samples too. Gate on a threshold with margin , never exact equality, see below. Ragas implements exactly this (decompose answer into claims → check each against context → faithfulness = grounded claims / total claims). You can hand-roll the same shape with one extra API call in the shape your harness already makes. 5 · Retrieval-confidence gating: don't answer from weak evidence Before generating, look at the top chunk's similarity score. Below a threshold, the context is probably junk, so don't generate ; abstain or route to a fallback. The threshold is empirical: read the score distribution of your golden hits vs misses and cut where they separate. This stops the pipeline from dressing up a bad retrieval as a confident answer. 6 · Human-in-the-loop & the feedback loop Whatever the automation doesn't catch, log. Every abstention and every low-faithfulness answer goes to your trace (you already wired Langfuse). That log is not exhaust, it's your work queue : each abstention is a question the corpus should answer but can't. Fix it (add the doc, re-chunk, add a golden question) and the gap shrinks. This is what makes the answer gap close over time instead of sitting constant at 2/20 forever. The CI gate: how you catch “the LLM gave its own answer” Lesson 07 left this exactly half-answered. It correctly said: generation runs at temperature=1 (the GPT-5 tier rejects 0.0 ), so a commit gate on answer text would flake: you can't diff a non-deterministic string. True. But that is not a reason to skip the gate. You don't gate on the answer's wording. You gate on stable properties of the answer, aggregated into rates. Rates are robust to wording variance; that's the whole trick. Gate Rule (example) Runs Trap-set hallucination Questions whose answer is not in the corpus (Paris, generalized). Correct = abstain. Hallucination count must be 0. Every commit: the abstention check is a cheap string match, so it's fast and deterministic enough . Faithfulness floor Mean faithfulness across the golden set ≥ threshold (e.g. 4.0/5), and no regression vs last run. Nightly: it's a paid judge call per question. Abstention band Abstention rate on the real golden set stays within [lo, hi] , flags both a newly-timid and a newly-reckless model. Nightly. The key insight: why a trap set beats a content diff A trap set is a handful of questions you know the corpus can't answer. The right behavior is always the same regardless of wording, abstain , so you check a property (“did it decline?”), not a string. That property is stable under temperature=1 : a well-grounded model abstains every time; a model that sometimes abstains and sometimes fabricates is exactly the flakiness you want the gate to catch, not hide. So the trap-set gate is both cheap enough for every commit and the most direct possible test of “is the LLM inventing answers?” Make the abstention check robust: string-match your sentinel ( "I don't have that information" ) first; only fall back to a judge call if the sentinel is absent but the answer might still be a decline. Cheap path first, judge as backstop. The split falls straight out of what each half is : retrieval metrics are free, deterministic, cached-fast → every commit . The faithfulness judge and abstention band are paid, slow, sampled → nightly, gating the release . The trap set is the bridge, it costs a few generation calls but tests the one thing you can't afford to let regress silently, so it earns a place on every commit. Where guardrails proper fit (forward pointer) Everything above defends answer quality . A separate layer defends against adversarial input/output: prompt injection, PII leakage, excessive agency. That's the OWASP LLM Top 10 , and it gets its own lesson (L10). Don't conflate the two: faithfulness stops the model being wrong ; guardrails stop it being abused . Defend it in one line “I can't build a RAG system that's always right, nobody can; honest RAG tops out well under 1.0. So I build one that fails safe and tells me when it's wrong. Grounded prompting and forced abstention turn a retrieval miss into ‘I don't know’ instead of a fabrication; a faithfulness judge grades every answer against only its context, so an ungrounded claim fails even when it's accidentally true; and a trap set of unanswerable questions gates CI on hallucination count, because generation is non-deterministic, I gate on stable rates , not on the answer text. Every abstention is logged, and that log is the queue of gaps I close in the corpus next.”
